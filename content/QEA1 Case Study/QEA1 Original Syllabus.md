## What is Quantitative Engineering Analysis?

Using quantitative analysis of models and data to make choices is critical for success in the engineering design process.  Whether you are choosing an appropriate material, sizing a component, determining the effect of certain influences on your design, or optimizing your design within a parameter space, you often need to obtain (through experiment or calculation) and interpret quantitative information to inform your decisions.  There are many different approaches to getting and interpreting the data you need to make your decision: you may wish to conduct an experiment, do a rough estimation, perform a detailed calculation based on mathematical models of your system, or create a computer simulation. If you want to engineer effectively, you must be able to choose and use appropriate quantitative approaches for a given situation. This course is part of a sequence of courses to help you develop these skills.

In QEA1, we'll be learning about quantitative engineering analysis in the context of facial recognition. However, the class is about more than learning math and engineering content.

We have intentionally designed the QEA experience with the goal of success for every student. To accomplish this, we need you as active participants in designing this class and making it the best it can be. We want to emphasize four philosophical points:

## Course Structure

### The Big Picture

This class is intended to help you understand how to undertake a quantitative analysis of an engineering problem, so that when you are faced with a complicated engineering challenge, you can break it up into well-defined questions and understand which tools will be useful in helping you to solve this problem. This class is about quantitative design, and being able to understand what is 'under the hood' of computational tools that you will be using in your everyday engineering world. This course is designed around one main challenge (facial recognition), but will be broken up into modules to help you learn the key building blocks and understand how these concepts can be applied to other engineering problems. 

### In-Class Work

Each day there will be an in-class document that provides a set of readings and activities for the class period. Generally speaking, you will work through this document collaboratively with your team, with faculty and course assistants dropping in to provide support. In-class activities may include, but are certainly not limited to, debriefs of the previous homework assignment, micro-lectures, exercises, and project work.

### Homework Assignments

There will be a homework assignments that dive deeper into key conceptual material that was introduced in class. We will often provide some of the solutions along with the homework assignments in order to better facilitate self-directed learning. There will be two deliverables associated with each homework assignment.

- Deliverable 1: a Canvas survey that asks you to evaluate your engagement on the assignment, reflect on things that went well for you, identify areas for improvement, and list particular concepts that you are still struggling with.  You will also submit your work for the assignment, but its correctness will not factor into your grade.  We will look at these from time-to-time to get a better sense of how folks are doing with the material (but it will not be used to evaluate you as an individual). The weekly homework and corresponding survey will typically be due at 10:59PM on Monday evenings, but some assignments may be due on Thursday evenings.
    
- Deliverable 2: a diagnostic quiz on Canvas associated with each assignment and tied to specific learning objectives for the course.  This will allow you to test whether you've understood some of the key concepts.  That said, the quizzes do not test every concept that is important on the assignment, so it is important to work through all of the problems on the assignment (and not focus only on the quiz material).  You should complete the diagnostic quiz individually (even if you are working through the assignment with someone else).  This is important to help you build confidence that you are mastering the material. The weekly diagnostic quiz will typically be due at 10:59PM on Tuesday or Friday evenings. If you feel good about the homework, you can certainly do the diagnostic quiz earlier in the week; however, by having them due a day later than the homework, you also have a chance to ask more questions during our class debriefs. You can retake the quizzes, but there will be a time buffer before you can take it again. This is to help you pause and reflect on the material and revisit it later (spaced repetition is helpful for many types of learning). 
    

We encourage a workflow similar to

1. Read the whole assignment at a surface level. Identify material you may have seen before, and concepts that are new to you. Note sections where you might want to seek additional resources, either written or in person.
    
2. Attempt the exercises without the solutions (work together or independently). If you are really struggling, move on, often the best way to approach an assignment is not linearly. Mark exercises you don’t understand so you can come back to them.
    
3. Go to office hours to get help and co-work. 
    
4. Finish the assignment to the best of your ability.
    
5. Submit the homework survey on Canvas to reflect on your approach to the assignment.
    
6. When the solutions are posted, review your homework with the solutions and identify areas of confusion.
    
7. Take the diagnostic quiz (this is an individual activity). 
    
8. If you did not answer all questions correctly on the diagnostic quiz, identify the learning outcomes that need more attention and then retake the learning goal specific quiz.
    

### Project

For each QEA module, you will be working with one or more colleagues to solve engineering challenges that require a quantitative solution.  In some modules this will take the form of one large challenge, and in other modules you may solve a series of challenges. For this fall, we'll just have one project at the end of the semester, with a deliverable of a poster presentation. The projects give you a chance to synthesize the knowledge you've learned from the module, put your own stamp on the work by pushing the project in interesting directions as you see fit, and hopefully have a lot of fun in the process by creating something you are truly proud of.

### Table Assignments

Generally for class, you will be randomly assigned to a room and table team within that room.  We randomize team assignments so students have the opportunity to work with a variety of classmates and different faculty throughout the semester.  At some point in the semester, we may give you some choice around teaming, but we ask you to start with an openness and flexibility. 

### Collaboration

A lot of people learn best working with others, and we encourage you to do this if it works for you. That means that it's fine to talk to a bunch of people about how to approach an exercise, but you should be able to confidently explain the concepts, and you should take the conceptual quiz individually. On projects, you'll be working with other folks, but each person should be actively involved with every aspect of the project (rather than, for example, one person doing coding and another doing the write-up).  We ask you to work together so you will learn from each other and learn to collaborate.  We (the teaching team) are also excited to work with you all, so please come to our office hours (where we will be waiting patiently, hoping to see your lovely faces so we can work with you to help you better understand the material!).

# Module 1: Introduction to Linear Algebra Through Facial Recognition and Introduction to Facial Recognition Through Linear Algebra

### The Big Picture

- When designing facial recognition systems (and many other AI and computing technologies), it is critical to keep ethical, contextual, and societal considerations at the forefront. Throughout the semester, we will examine a few frameworks to analyze and discuss the issues
    
- The analogy of adding together different amounts of building blocks to represent data of a certain type (images of faces in our examples) is a core idea of linear algebra that underlies many technologies throughout engineering, including machine learning, signal processing, numerical analysis, chemical engineering, and quantum mechanics, just to name a few
    
- In order to improve fluency with matrices and vectors, it is helpful to regularly ask questions like, "what are the dimensions of that data matrix?", "what does one row correspond to?", "what does one column correspond to", "if C=AB, what is the size of C and how do I interpret its elements in the context of the application/data?"

# Module 2: Modeling with Systems of Linear Algebraic Equations

### The Big Picture

- Many real-world situations can be modeled by systems of linear equations
    
- There are three equivalent ways to write down these systems, each with their own interpretations
    

- As m separate equations/constraints of n unknown variables (e.g., the first constraint is a11x1+a12x2+...a1nxn=b1, where x1,x2,...,xn are the unknown variables and everything else is known). In words, this can be interpreted as, "Can we find a series of values for the n unknowns such that all m constraints are satisfied?"
    
- As a matrix equation Ax=b, where A is a known mxn matrix, x is an unknown nx1 vector, and b is a known mx1 vector. In words, this can be interpreted as, "Does there exist an n-vector x such that A times x is equal to the m-vector b?"
    
- As a vector equation x1v1+x2v2+...+xnvn=b, where vi is the ith column of the matrix A and xi is the ith (scalar) element of the vector x. In words, this can be interpreted as, "can we find a linear combination of the building blocks v1, v2,...,vn that add up to the vector b?"
    

- Linear algebra concepts like vector space, range, basis, span, and linear dependence/independence help us answer questions like, "Does the given system of linear equations have a solution? If so, is it unique?"

# Module 3: Decomposition, Orthogonality, Orthogonal Projection, Least Squares Solutions, Representing Data with Matrices, and Correlation

### The Big Picture

- We've talked a lot about writing vectors (representing images, faces, audio signals, etc.) as linear combinations of building blocks. Now we start to ask the question, "What makes for a good choice of building blocks to use?" That is, "Which ingredients do you want to have in your cabinet?" The answer will always depend to some extent on the specific application, but throughout the remainder of the semester, we'll examine a few helpful properties of building blocks.
    
- The first desirable property is orthonormality: the building blocks each have length equal to 1 and they are all orthogonal to each other.
    

- The answer to the decomposition question, "How much of each building block basis vector do we need to make a particular vector b?" typically requires us to solve an inverse problem Ax=b. However, when the columns of A (the building blocks) are orthornormal, this question is much easier to answer, because A-1=AT, and we can find the amount of each building block required by doing a more numerically efficient orthogonal projection onto each building block.
    

- The same concept of orthogonal projection is key to understanding the question, "What happens if our system of linear algebraic equations is overconstrained and does not have a solution? Can we find a solution that is 'closest' to satisfying all of the constraints?" We'll examine this idea in the context of least squares problems.
    
- Two vectors (series of values of two different quantities) are positively correlated if, on average, when a given element (say the 4th entry) of the first vector is larger (smaller) than the mean of that first vector, the corresponding element (say the 4th entry) of the second vector is also larger (smaller) than the mean of that second vector. They are negatively correlated, also called anti-correlated, if when one entry is larger than its mean, the corresponding entry in the other vector is smaller than its mean on average. When there is on average no relation, they are called uncorrelated.
    
- Correlation does not imply causation. Two vectors can be correlated without having any causality relationship (e.g., [the divorce rate in Maine has been correlated with the per capita consumption of margarine](https://www.tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations?)
    
- [Links to an external site.](https://www.tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations?)
    
- )
    
- The correlation coefficient between two vectors is between -1 (exactly negatively correlated) and 1 (exactly positively correlated), with 0 being uncorrelated. The correlation matrix between n vectors puts all of the pairwise correlation coefficients into a (symmetric) matrix (e.g., the ijth entry is the correlation coefficient between the ith and jth vectors). The diagonal elements of a correlation matrix are always equal to 1, because a vector is perfectly correlated with itself.
    
- For image processing (including facial recognition), we can use the idea of correlation in two different ways:
    

1. Given a series of n images, how correlated are the ith image and the jth image (when we first vectorize each image)?
    
2. Given a series of n images, how correlated is the vector of the (i,j) pixels (formed by taking the (i,j) pixel of all n images) with the vector of the (k,l) pixels (formed by taking the (k,l) pixel of all n images)?

# Module 4: Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and Eigendecomposition

### The Big Picture

- One of the big questions of the course is, "How do we choose useful building blocks?" We've already seen one property that is often desirable: they form an orthornomal set. # Module 5: Principal Component Analysis

### The Big Picture

- One of the big questions of the course is, "How do we choose useful building blocks?" Given a data set with m observations in the rows and a high number n of features in the columns, it is often desirable to create building blocks that are ordered from those that capture the most variation in the data to those that carry the least variation in the data.
    
- The eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the centered data - called principal component vectors - do exactly this, with the eigenvector  associated with the largest eigenvalue  being the one that capture the most variation, and the eigenvector  associated with the smallest eigenvalue  being the one that capture the least variation.
    
- These principal component vectors form an orthonormal basis (because they are the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix).
    
- Thus, to find out how much of a chosen principal component j is used to build a chosen row/observation i of the data matrix, we just take the dot product between that row and that principal component vector  and call this weight .
    
- Then we can write row i (expressed as a column vector) exactly as .
